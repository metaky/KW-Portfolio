## Design Context

### Users
This site is a personal portfolio for Kyle Wegner. Its audience is split across people who may hire or collaborate with Kyle for marketing strategy, disability advocacy, disability-centered tool building, or AI strategy and implementation.

Visitors may arrive with different needs, but the job of the interface is consistent: help them quickly understand Kyle's range, trust his judgment, feel the human purpose behind the work, and see a clear path to reach out.

### Brand Personality
The brand should feel welcoming, pleasant, and direct. It should communicate strategic credibility without becoming corporate, technical fluency without becoming cold, and advocacy without becoming heavy-handed.

The emotional goal is calming and immersive. The site should invite people in, make the work feel thoughtful and grounded, and give disabled users an obvious sense that their needs were considered from the beginning.

### Aesthetic Direction
The current visual system is a warm editorial portfolio with soft watercolor accents. Preserve the existing foundation: Playfair Display for expressive headings, Inter for clean reading, forest green as the primary anchor, off-white surfaces, gentle blue/yellow/green accents, rounded cards, soft shadows, and light motion.

Future updates should make the site feel more immersive only when the content supports it. A previously favored direction is the "Living Story Hero": a signature, scroll-linked homepage introduction built around presence, duality, and purpose. Do not implement that kind of large hero treatment until the homepage messaging is bold enough to carry it.

Avoid generic SaaS styling, loud tech-startup gloss, dark aggressive visuals, overly busy layouts, and anything that makes the portfolio feel like a template. The work should feel specific to Kyle: strategic, useful, human, and accessible.

### Design Principles
1. Lead with hireability and human purpose. Every page should help a visitor understand what Kyle can do, why it matters, and how to start a conversation.
2. Make accessibility visible, not hidden. Disabled users should be able to tell from the interface itself that accommodation is a core design value, with clear structure, readable type, strong contrast, generous spacing, keyboard-friendly controls, reduced-motion respect, and no reliance on color alone.
3. Stay warm, calm, and direct. Use pleasant visual detail and plain language, but avoid overexplaining, overdecorating, or making simple actions feel complicated.
4. Keep the editorial-watercolor identity. Build from the existing serif/sans typography, forest green anchor, soft off-white surfaces, watercolor accents, rounded cards, and gentle motion instead of replacing the personality with a generic system.
5. Let immersive moments earn their space. Larger motion or storytelling features should deepen the portfolio's message, not act as decoration.
